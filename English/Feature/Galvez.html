The Cost of Survival

Written by Nevaeh Emmanuel R. Galvez

Picture this: you are another student who is almost done with class and waiting for the dismissal bell to ring. For most people, that sound signals relief, the end of another school day. But for others, packing up their bags and leaving school does not bring peace. The weight they carry in their bags is nothing compared to the tension and anxiety wrapped tightly around their minds. Threats of hostility or violence change how a student interacts with the world long after dismissal. They can’t help but constantly look for potential escape exits, positioning themselves in a way that no one else can sneak up behind them. Sometimes, they struggle to form meaningful relationships because of deep skepticism towards other people and fellow students. They may even feel the necessity to have a weapon close by at all times. All these measures and self-built protocols are symptoms that students have exhibited or done because of traumatic events, such as school shootings or bullying, that essentially changed them.

A school is supposed to be a place of safety where students get to learn about themselves and the world around them while picking up on lessons that they will need for the future. If a school becomes a place of danger caused by constant bullying or school shootings, a student will start viewing normal situations and places as potential hazard zones or traps waiting to spring. Recent data originating from the Philippine Mental Health Association and Department of Health show that over 12.5 million Filipinos experience mental health conditions, with anxiety and depression being the most prevalent among youth. Research that tracked long-term outcomes shows that bullying during childhood causes depression, anxiety disorders, emotional distress, and even suicidal ideation that will remain in the years to come (Takizawa et al., 2014). The suffering that the impacted students underwent in their early development years as children essentially conditioned them to stay alert to others and their own surroundings.

Students begin to question their school place, confusing environmental hostility with personal incompetence. People who experienced school shootings and relentless peer harassment often feel insecure, even in neutral environments like classrooms, coffee shops or even restaurants. This is called hypervigilance, where even something as basic as loud noise, a door slam, or a fire alarm can cause quick physical reactions like instinctively searching for the nearest exit, an increased heart rate, and cold perspiration. Longitudinal studies on economics and human capital reveal that being exposed to severe school violence events creates constant disruptions in a student’s academic path, such as avoiding school more often and having disrupted basic thinking skills and long-term mental health instability (Cabral et al., 2020). The burden of hypervigilance will consume a person as it drains their daily energy, making everyday routines a constant uphill battle.

Every student deserves to feel safe in a learning environment like a school, much like their own homes. That safety isn’t just about locked doors, more security guards, or rules, but about a more stable and caring learning environment. The community around the affected individuals should help them feel like they belong here. Teachers who listen first before correcting without reason, fellow students and peers who choose empathy instead of exclusion, and institutional systems that prioritize every child’s dignity and safety. For students affected by school violence or bullying, the path to healing won’t be easy or quick, but spotting the source of anxiety is the first crucial step in reclaiming control. To defeat their own mental struggle against trauma and personal thoughts, they need external support such as specialized trauma-informed therapy, peer support networks, and to learn that they should ground themselves in the present instead of lingering in the past. Even after giving them the necessary tools to become better and feel supported, it is up to them, who once were victims of violent human-made disasters, to dictate what happens next in their lives.
